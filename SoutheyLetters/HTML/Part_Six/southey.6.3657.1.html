3657.1 Robert Southey to John Rickman, 24 March [1821]1

My dear R.

I receive amusing accounts of my hexameters.2 Women, as I expected, readily perceive, & like the metre. Young poets admire it beyond its deserts.3 Many persons abstain from an opinion till they see which way the wind will set. Wynn says that certainly he does not dislike it so much as he expected. The literatuli I hear express their astonishment that I should have ventured to attack Lord Byron, & anticipate a tremendous visitation of his wrath on my devoted head.4 – Among my peers, I may in Landors words of old, call for a division & count a majority.5

I expect by every post to hear of an explosion in the Prussian States.6 – There scarcely seems a chance of escape for France & Belgium.7 The only possibility (a poor one it is) is that some speedy & signal success of the Austrians may decide the business in Naples by proving that in a maro the people care nothing about a revolution, in which the soldiers are moved by speculative lawyers, ambitious nobles, & atheistical priests.8 Yet in other times how one should have wished to see Italy shake off the Austrian yoke!

Europe never wanted a Master Mind so much as at the Congress of Vienna, when all this evil might have been averted.9

– Have you read Michaelis’s “Commentaries on the Laws of Moses”?10 – If you have not, there are very few books which would please you more.

I am sorry to learn from Mrs Gee11 that Miss Emma12 has been seriously ill. I do not like to think of her as otherwise than in health & happiness, – as she was in <during> our Scotch tour which had no other unpleasant circumstance about it than that like all human pleasures – it came to an end. I request Mrs R. will make my kindest remembrances to her, when next she writes.

We are tolerably well here, & Cuthbert continues to thrive & to promise well.

God bless youRS.

March 24.

Notes

1.  MS: Huntington Library, RS 422. ALS; 3p.
Unpublished. Back

2.  Southey’s A Vision of Judgement (1821). Back

3.  See Southey to John Abraham Heraud, 15 March 1821, The Collected Letters of Robert Southey. Part Six, Letter 3653 and Southey to Ebenezer Elliott, 25 March 1821, The Collected Letters of Robert Southey, Part Six, Letter 3658. Back

4.  Southey had attacked ‘the Satanic school’ of poetry in the ‘Preface’ to A Vision of Judgement (London, 1821), pp. xvii–xxii. Back

5.   Walter Savage Landor, Gebir; a Poem, in Seven Books (London, 1798), p. ii: ‘If there are, now in England, ten men of taste and genius who will applaud my Poem, I declare myself fully content. I will call for a division; I shall count a majority’. The book was no. 1030 in the sale catalogue of Southey’s library. Back

6.   Revolutions in Spain, Portugal and Naples in 1820 led Southey to fear further revolts. In the areas of the Rhineland that were incorporated into Prussia in 1815, local elites were fighting a long battle with the government in Berlin to defend their local government system and legal code. But there was no revolutionary outbreak until 1848. Back

7.   Revolution was avoided in both France and Belgium, but only until 1830. Back

8.  Austrian troops crushed the revolution in Naples easily, entering the capital city on 23 March 1821. Back

9.   The Congress of Vienna (1814–1815) had settled the borders of Europe after the Napoleonic wars. Southey disapproved of many of its policies, including the decision to keep Italy divided between a number of small states, most of them under Austrian domination. Back

10.  Johann David Michaelis (1717–1791), Mosaisches Recht (1770–1775), translated as Commentaries on the Laws of Moses (1814). Back

11.  Fanny Gee (c. 1772–1846), wife of Captain George Gee (d. 1827), of Wraxall, Somerset. The Gees were renting Ivy Cottage at Rydal. Captain Gee was the son of Thomas Gee, a Bristol merchant, and had been an old schoolmate of Southey’s in Bristol. He seems to have played an important backstage role in organising the Lowther family’s election contests in Westmorland in 1818, 1820 and 1826. Back

12.  Miss Pigott had accompanied Southey and Rickman and his family on their tour of Scotland in August-September 1819. It is difficult to be sure of her identity, but she might have been Emma Pigott (dates unknown), younger daughter and co-heiress of James Pigott (d. 1822) of Fitz-Hall, Iping, Sussex. If this identification is correct, Miss Pigott survived her illness and married, in 1824, Edward Brice Bunny (d. 1867) of Speen Hill, Berkshire. Back
