3486.1 Robert Southey to [Unknown Correspondent], [26 May 1820]*

My dear Sir

I have a distinct recollection of you, as you may perceive by this token, that you made enquiry of me concerning the author of Count Julian,1 & were one of the few persons who properly appreciated his merits as a poet.

It would give me great pleasure to renew & improve my acquaintance with you. And I should also have been much gratified by an opportunity of becoming known to Sir William Curtis,2 respecting as I do the course & the manliness of his political conduct. But I am too deeply engaged to allow of this. I have to go thro much business of many kinds, & do not expect to have any thing like a feeling of rest till I seat myself in the mail coach for Penrith, in the last week of June.

Whenever you may visit our part of the country again, remember that you need no introduction.

& believe me my dear siryours faithfullyRobert Southey.

I am going to Cambridge tomorrow for the sake of seeing our friend Neville

Notes

Endorsement: R Southey
MS: Special Collections Research Center, University of Kentucky Libraries. ALS; 3p.
Unpublished.
Dating note: Dating from contents – Southey left London for Cambridge on 27 May 1820. Back

1.  Count Julian: a Tragedy (1812), by Walter Savage Landor. The work was published anonymously. Back

2.   Sir William Curtis, 1st Baronet (1752–1829; DNB), merchant, banker and prominent figure in the government of the City of London. He was one of the MPs for the City 1790–1818 and 1820–1826. Curtis was a consistent supporter of the government. At a celebration dinner on 6 April 1820, following his re-election as an MP for the City after a bitter struggle with his Whig opponents, he had denounced radical agitators and praised the Ministry. Curtis was also a supporter of George IV in his battle with his estranged wife, Queen Caroline (1768–1821; DNB). Back
