4206. Robert Southey to [[John May]], [c. 27 June 1824] *

Keswick. 27 June. 1824My dear friend

I was truly glad to hear from you once more, after so long a silence. You must be much better informed than I can possibly be, with the state of affairs in Brazil; as far as I can judge, the aspects are more favourable than they were, both there & in Portugal. Both the Emp. of Brazil1 & his brother D Miguel2 appear to be great brutes & blackguards, – & yet to possess a certain strength of character which may work off the bad matter. Of the former I have heard that one of his royal recreations was to ride a negro with spurs.

He has however acted with vigour, & if he can content himself with Brazil, giving up all hope of uniting the two countries under one government, I think he may hold it, by help of his fleet, & by making it the interest of the Representatives3 to support him, – I mean bestowing an hundredth part of the money in buying them, which it would cost to suppress a revolt. He must govern by iron or by gold, till the people are in a state to be ruled by reason. The golden system is the cheapest & the best.

This ought to be his policy. The K of Portugal4 is now pursuing what is his true course. Reviving the old Cortes, & with the old forms, preparing I trust to bring back the realities of justice. I have long been clearly of opinion that in Sp. & P. the true mode of reforming government, is to do this: & to pass a Habeas Corpus act.5 They are not ripe for any thing farther.

In my judgement your concerns as far as they depend upon political movements ought to be more promising than they were. How heartily should I rejoice to hear that you were freed from this long & painful anxiety axxxxt <but> I never can forgive your brother6 for having involved you in it.

My brother Toms subscription7 is coming on prosperously. He has very nearly half the number required, & good hope from other quarters which have not yet sent in their returns

Poor Mrs Tonkin8 – but why should I say poor? – when I verily believe that if suicide were held allowable by Christian principles not half so many persons would reach threescore & ten, as now drag about the burthen of old age. The worst of all calamities is that of living too long.

I send you herewith the fruits of a good resolution which if I can keep up to it, will bring my task to an end within a reasonable time.9 – It will be a good post–obit, & ought to be worth 2000£, – 1500 would readily be offered for it. You are aware that I make a transcript which will be found in a state fit for the press.

Before I left London Murray offered me 700£ for the B of the Church,10 which I refused, telling him he must well know that of <all> the authors with whom he was concerned, I was the one whom, all circumstances considered, he had hitherto paid the worst, – a fact too palpable for him to deny. – When I tell you that he had originally offered me <500£> for this very book in one small volume – that is – not more than one third of its present extent, – you will see that I had reason to refuse his proposal, & to feel more resentment than I expressed.

He promised to write to me concerning some plans which we had long talked of – but I have only had two lines from him since my return, – & those to say he had sent a second edition to press.11 – I have drawn upon him meantime to the amount of 310£, – my half profits upon the first edition ought to be more than this – but I do not yet know what number he printed. For Wesley12 (an edition of 1500) I had 400£. But of the after editions of the B C. I x am to have two thirds.

Thus the matter stands at present.

The first sheet of my second vol. of the P. War13 is lying before me. It will be published in the winter, – & the payment as soon as I receive it shall be forthwith transferred to you. And I shall have no need to make any drawbacks from it.

God bless you –

Yrs affectionatelyRS.

I had nearly forgotten to answer your question concerning the books which elucidate our Eccl. History.14 The two works which profess that to embrace that subject exclusively are Fullers,15 & Jeremy Collier’s.16 The first will I hope be reprinted at the Clarendon Press, for with all its manifold imperfections it contains much matter for which no other authority can now be found the record of the Convocation17 having been destroyed. & it has moreover all the inimitable charm of Fullers manner. – Collier is coarse & clumsy – - a bigot – on the right side. – It was necessary that I should have both at hand, but you would find upon investigation that I have drawn my materials from other sources, Collier’s never lay open before me, – & Fuller’s I believe only in the reign of James I.18

For facts relating to the history of the Church the most common books are Kennetts Parochial Antiquities, & his Case of Impropriations.19 H Wharton Defence of Pluralities.20 Stavelys History of Churches.21

Bede22 & the Acta Sanctorum23 were my resources for the early history – with Wm of Malmesbury.24

To my sorrow I had no original authorities for the life of Becket,25 except such as were in the App. to Ld Lyttletons history.26 Berrington27 is the best historian of those times, – indeed much the fairest of all the English Romanists in his writings

We come now to Lewis’s Life Lives of Wicliffe & of Bp Pecock28 – I had also Baber’s Life of Wiclif prefixed to his New Testament29 – & Fox’s Martyrs.30

Then came the whole series of Strypes laborious compilations,31 – in which I believe nothing has escaped me, tho in this work I have not made use of the 50th part of my references to them. – Fox. Burnett.32 thDr Wordsworths Ecc. Biography.33 Rushworth & Nalson34 – (the former I must observe is not known as he deserves to be for a great rogue, – but he has perfectly convinced me that a writer may deserve to be punished as severely for his sins of omission, as for direct falsehood.) All the lives of Laud35 – Hackets Life of Ab Williams36 – a much more important book (in spite of its odd, but very amusing stile) than it is generally known to be. A great deal has been drawn from Tracts published during the Civil Wars, – of which I found a rich collection at Lowther. – In short from all histories, biograph memoirs, &c of all parties on which I could lay hands. And I need x not tell you that my previous knowledge of monastic history was of ‘great use.’

Every body has cried out for references. I will give them as I find leisure for doing it, in some future edition: & I will do for this reason, that when the references are given, the reader who xxx is diligent enough to refer to them, may see how faithfully I have followed <represented> the facts & how completely the composition is my own.

Endorsement: No 238/ Copied [Michigan State MS, see below]; No. 238 [1824/ Robert Southey/ Keswick 27th June/ recd. 1st Jule/ ansd. 27th Oct. [Harry Ransom MS, see below].
MS: At some point in its history the letter was separated into two parts, which are now split between two archives. The first part (everything from ‘Keswick. 27 June. 1824’ to the closing signature) is tipped into the first volume of a copy of Southey’s The Book of the Church, 2 vols (London, 1824) that once belonged to John Coleridge Frampton May (1837-1902) and is now in Stephen O. Murray and Keelung Hong Special Collections, Michigan State University Libraries, XX BX5055.S7 1824. ALS; 4p. The second part (everything after the signature) is in the Robert Southey Collection, Harry Ransom Center, The University of Texas at Austin. AL; 4p.
Previously published: The section of the manuscript now in Michigan State has never been published. The section of manuscript in the Harry Ransom Center appeared in John Wood Warter (ed.), Selections from the Letters of Robert Southey, 4 vols (London, 1856), III, pp. 429–430. Back

1.  Peter I (1798–1834; Emperor of Brazil 1822–1831 and King of Portugal March–May 1826). He had put himself at the head of the movement for Brazilian independence from Portugal and had defeated Portuguese forces in Brazil in 1822–1824. Back

2.  Michael I (1802–1866; King of Portugal 1828–1834), younger brother of Peter I. In May 1824 he had been exiled to Vienna after attempting to seize power in Portugal. Back

3.  Peter I had summoned a Constituent Assembly in May 1823 to draft a new constitution for Brazil, but dissolved this body in November 1823 and produced his own constitution in March 1824. Back

4.  John VI (1767–1826; King of Portugal 1816–1826), father of Peter I and Michael I. In 1823 he had abolished the liberal Constitution and Cortes in Portugal created by the revolution of 1820 and promised to restore the form of the old Cortes, which had last met in 1698 and consisted of the three orders of nobility, Church and representatives of the towns. Back

5.  The Habeas Corpus Act (1679) prevented detention without trial. Back

6.  William May (1785–1849), John May’s younger brother, was in charge of the family’s business interests in Brazil, which were in dire trouble. Back

7.  Southey was collecting subscriptions for his brother’s proposed Chronological History of the West Indies (1827). Back

8.  Mary Tonkin, nee Windsor (d. 1824), widow of the Lisbon merchant William Tonkin (dates unknown). Southey had first met Mrs Tonkin on his trip to Portugal in 1796 and had become a close friend of her and her children. Back

15.  Thomas Fuller (1607/8–1661; DNB), The Church-History of Britain: from the Birth of Jesus Christ until the Year MDCXLVIII, with the History of the University of Cambridge and of Waltham-abby in Essex, Founded by King Harold (1655), no. 1190 in the sale catalogue of Southey’s library. Oxford University Press did publish a new edition, edited by John Sherren Brewer (1810–1879; DNB), but not until 1845. Back

16.  Jeremy Collier (1650–1726; DNB), An Ecclesiastical History of Great Britain, Chiefly of England: from the First Planting of Christianity, to the End of the Reign of Charles the Second (1708–1714). Collier was a High Churchman and refused to accept the Revolution of 1688, eventually becoming a non-juring bishop in 1713. Back

17.  The Convocations of Canterbury and York were the assemblies of bishops and clergy of the two Provinces of the Church of England. They approved revisions to canon law and agreed to taxes on the clergy, but their meetings had been formalities since 1717. Their proceedings can be gleaned from medieval archbishops’ registers and Southey could have consulted David Wilkins (1685–1745; DNB), Concilia Magnae Britanniae et Hiberniae a Synodo Verolamiensi A.D. 446 ad Londinensam A.D. 1717 (1737). Back
