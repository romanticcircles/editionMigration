4094. Robert Southey to Bertha Southey, 26 November 18231

Wednesday morning 26 Nov. 1823My dear Bertha

I take little Roberts2 desk (which he is proud of lending me) upon my knees, before the fire in the breakfast room, that I may get as far as I can thro a letter to your B-ship, before Mr Westall comes to breakfast with us. On Saturday morning I walked from Streatham, & had like poor Cupn, only two breakfasts that day, one with Bedford, the other with Wynn. We dined with Lady Malet,3 good part of the morning being uncomfortably disposed of to the deaf & dumb painter,4 who is thought to be making an excellent likeness of my Beautyship. The whole of Sunday, from half past eight in the morning till nine at night EMay & I past at Dr Hughes’s, & I shall leave her to describe the music at St Pauls, & the fine effect of the monuments there by gaslight, with the aweful darkness of the dome above. Dr Stoddart5 came there in the evening. Monday EMay <& I> went I to call on the Lambs6 at Colebrook Cottage, close by the New River at Islington.7 A more abominable situation I never saw & cannot conceive, – & yet because it had been a pleasant one in his childhood – & he remembered it with pleasure,8 he it seems had fixed his heart upon living there. The new river is filthy, green & apparently stagnant, the garden which is of tolerable size is surrounded by houses & there are yards als very near full of unhappy cows who live knee deep in their own litter, & a population of pigs whose stench is pestilential. I would rather be hanged than live there. We called on your Aunts9 as we returned, & we dined at Mr Wynns where Bedford met us Edith will relate how pleasant a visit this was, how much she likes the two eldest Miss Wynns, what delightful children the others are,10 & she will tell Cuthbert of the two squirrels which run about the room, & about the people in it, – the prettiest creatures you can imagine.

Yesterday I went to Mr Westalls & got there just two hours after the birth of his third son.11 Then I went down to Westminster to settle <with Wynn> about Ediths going to the play with his family when the King goes, which will be either tomorrow or Monday.12 We dined at Mr Jacobs,13 with whose eldest daughter you will please to tell your mother, I fell as much in love as it is proper <allowable> for me to do. She is the sort of woman that Miss Rickman14 was, but handsomer, & just in her full bloom. To day I have an appointment with an American Bishop at twelve where John Coleridge will meet me if he can.15 EMay goes with her Uncle & Aunt to dine at Mrs Cooksons16 – & I dine with Bedford, where I shall meet Wynn, & probably my old schoolfellow George Strachey, who has purchased Bownham in Gloucestershire, – the house in which Mr T. Smith lived, when I & poor Danvers visited him.17 – I shall look for Dr Bell this morning. He called here on Sunday, – & Dr & Mrs Hughes called on Monday, when we were out.

Elizas friend Mrs Hawkins18 who lives in Brooke Street now, has called on EMay, & invited her to dinner. By a blunder of the servants she was not let in tho Edith was at home.

Tomorrow my Uncle & Aunt, John May & John Coleridge dine here, the next day we remove to Rickmans. On the Tuesday following we dine & sleep at Sir Robert Inglis, – on the Friday leave Rickmans, & dine with Mr Longman at Hampstead, where we expect to meet Joanna Baillie. I am about to write to Mr Locker proposing to [MS missing] him next, & after a day or two with him, I shall return to Stre[MS missing] to finish my work, leaving Edith with Mrs Gonne or Lady Malet.

God bless you my dear daughteryr affectionate fatherRS.

Notes

1.  Address: [in another hand] London Twenty Seventh Nov./ 1823./ Miss Bertha Southey/ Keswick/ Cumberland/ From/ JRickman
Postmark: FREE/ 27 NO 27/ 1823/ +
MS: Keswick Museum and Art Gallery, WC 238. ALS; 4p.
Unpublished. Back

2.  Robert Southey, Jnr (b. 1817), eldest son of Henry Herbert Southey. Back

3.  Lady Susanna Malet, née Wales (1779–1868), widow of Sir Charles Malet, 1st Baronet (1752–1815; DNB), the maternal uncle of Emma Peachy (who was the first wife of Sir William Peachy). Malet’s London home was at 100 Gloucester Place, Portman Square, London. Back

4.  Samuel Lane (1780–1859; DNB), whose portrait of Southey is now at Balliol College, Oxford. Back

5.  John Stoddart (1773–1856; DNB), lawyer and journalist. He was editor of The Times (1814–1816), before starting his own daily paper, The New Times, which he edited 1817–1826. Back

6.  Charles and Mary Anne Lamb. Back

7.  The New River was an artificial waterway opened in 1813 to supply London with drinking water. It begins in Hertfordshire and originally terminated in the Clerkenwell area of Islington, although most of its water is now diverted into a reservoir at Stoke Newington. Back

8.  Colebrook Cottage was on the west side of the New River, an area which had remained relatively undeveloped into the 1790s. Back

9.  Eliza Fricker and Martha Fricker. Back

10.  Wynn had five daughters: Charlotte (1807–1869), Mary (1808–1869), Harriet (1812–1878), Emma (1814–1824) and Sidney (1818–1867); and two sons, Watkin (1816–1832) and Charles (1822–1896). Back

11.  Westall’s first two sons were: William Westall (1821–1901), later a clergyman and schoolmaster; and Richard Westall (b. 1822), who died in infancy. The third son, born in 1823, was also christened Richard, but seems to have been severely disabled. Westall had two further sons: Thomas Westall (b. 1825), later an engineer; and Robert Westall (1829–1908), who became an artist. Back

12.  George IV attended the Theatre Royal, Drury Lane, on Monday, 1 December 1823. It was his first visit for two years, because the interior of the theatre had been undergoing refurbishment. The play was Isaac Bickerstaffe (1733–1812?; DNB), The Hypocrite (1768). Back

13.  William Jacob (1762–1851; DNB), a merchant, statistician and former MP. He had three daughters. Back

14.  Ann Rickman (b. 1808). Back

15.  John Henry Hobart (1775–1830), Bishop of New York 1816–1830. He undertook a prolonged visit to Europe in 1823–1825 in an attempt to improve his health. Hobart later visited Southey in Keswick in August 1824. Back

16.  Possibly a relative of William Wordsworth’s uncle, William Cookson (d. 1820), Canon of Windsor 1792–1820; see Southey to Edith Southey, 12 [May 1820], The Collected Letters of Robert Southey. Part Six, Letter 3481. Back

17.  George Strachey bought Bownham House in 1821 for £5,825. He demolished the old house and built a new mansion in 1839–1849. Danvers and Southey had visited Thomas Smith at Bownham in March 1803. Back

18.  A Dr Hawkins lived at 37 Upper Brook St in 1824, and the caller may have been his wife. Back
